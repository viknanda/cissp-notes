# Business Continuity Planning

Disaster recovery has the goal of minimizing the effects of a disaster and taking the necessary steps to ensure that the resources, personnel and business processes are able to resume operation in a timely manner.

This is different from continuity planning, which deals with providing methods and procedures for dealing with longer-term outages and disasters.

Recovery planning involves developing a plan and preparing for disaster before it takes place in the hope of minimizing loss and ensuring the availability of critical systems and personnel.

Availability is extremely important in business continuity. Planning needs to include getting the right people to the right places, documenting the necessary configurations and making sure a redundant power supply is available.

Business continuity should be a part of the security policy and program and not an entity that stands off in a corner by itself. If it is properly integrated with other security issues, it has a better chance of being continually updated an improved upon. This can help ensure that security is thought of in each step of the business and disaster plans. Business continuity is a foundational piece of an effective security program.

Business impact analysis (BIA) is a crucial first step in business continuity planning. A BIA is performed at the beginning of business continuity planning to identify the areas that would suffer the greatest financial or operational loss in the event of a disaster or disruption. It identifies the company's critical systems needed for survival and estimates the outage time that can be tolerated by the company as a result of a disaster or disruption.

Loss criteria may contain:

- loss in reputation and public confidence

- loss in profits

- loss of competitive advantages

- increase in operational expenses

- violations of contract agreements

- violations of legal and regulatory requirements

- delayed income costs

- loss in revenue

- loss in productivity The outage time that can be endured by a company is referred to as the maximum tolerable downtime (MTD).

- Normal = 7 days

- Important = 72 hours

The following interrelation and interdependency issues should be researched and addressed in the business continuity plan:

- Define essential business functions and supporting departments

- Identify interdependencies between these functions and departments

- Discover all possible disruptions that could affect the mechanisms necessary to allow these departments to function together

- Identify and document potential threats that could disrupt interdepartmental communication

- Gather quantitative and qualitative information pertaining to those threats

- Provide alternative methods of restoring functionality and communication

- Provide a brief statement of rationale for each threat and corresponding information It is very important that management understands what the real risks are to the company, consequences of those risks and the potential loss value for each and every risk. Management further needs to make sure that the plans stay updated and represent the priorities of a company, which can change over time.

A very critical requirement of business continuity is to perform continual data backups of files as well as software that uses these files.

Establishing goals is especially important for business continuity and recovery plans. The definition of the goals helps direct the proper allocation of resources and tasks, develops necessary strategies and assists in economical justification of the plans and program overall.

Goals --- guide ---> Strategies --- guide ---> Actions --- accomplish ---> Goals Goals must contain certain key information:

- Responsibility – each individual involved with recovery and continuity should have his responsibilities spelled out in writing to ensure a clear understanding in a chaotic situation. Each tasks should be assigned to the most logical individual.

- Authority – in time of crisis, it is important to know who is in charge. Teamwork is important in these situations and almost every team does much better with an established and trusted leader. Clear-cut authority will aid in reducing confusion and increasing cooperation.

- Priorities – it is extremely critical to know what is critical versus what is nice to have. Priorities of departments, systems, information and programs must be established. The general priorities must be set by the management with the help of the different departments and IT staff.

- Implementation and testing – once a continuity plan is developed, it actually has to be put into action. It needs to be documented and put in places that are easily accssible in times of crisis. Dry runs and testing need to be done to walk people through different situations.

Phases of a Continuity Plan Project Initiation --> Business Impact Analysis --> Strategy Development --> Plan Development --> Implementation --> Testing <---> Maintenance The main plan must be enterprise-wide. In large corporations, individual departments should have their own disaster recovery and continuity plans because it would be hard to create one large plan that would provide enough granularity to be useful to each and every department. Each department's plan is then integrated with other departments' plans into the overall recovery plan for the company.

Nondisaster – a disruption in service as a result from a device malfunction or failure Disaster – an event that causes the entire facility to be unusable for a day or longer Catastrophe – a major disruption that destroys the facility altogether MTBF – mean time between failure MTTR – mean time to repair Hardware Backup options:

- Hot Site – a facility that is fully configured and would be ready to operate within a few hours. The only missing resources from a hot site are usually the data, which will be retrieved from a backup site and the people who will be processing the data. A hot site can support a short-or long-term outage. Most hot site facilities support annual tests that can be done by the company to ensure the site is functioning in the necessary state. This is the most expensive option in terms of offsite facilities and can have problems if a company requires proprietary or unusual hardware or software.

- Warm Site – these facilities are usually configured with some equipment, but not the actual computers.

A warm site is usually a hot site without the expensive equipment. It is less expensive than a hot site and can be up and running within an acceptable time period. It may be a better choice for companies that depend upon proprietary and unusual hardward and software because they will be bringing their own hardware and software with them to the site after the disaster hits. Annual testing is not available with warm site contracts.

- Cold Site – these facilities supply the basic environment, electrical wiring, air conditioning, plumbing and flooring, but it may take weeks to get the site activated and ready for work. The cold site can receive equipment but does not provide any. It is the least expensive option, but will take the most time and effort to actually get up and running right after a disaster.

A redundant site is a site owned and maintained by the company, hence the company need not pay subscription to anyone for using the site. A redundant site may be “hot” in nature.

Advantages of Hot Sites:

- Ready within hours for operations

- Highly available

- Usually used for short-term solutions, but available for longer stays

- Annual testing available Disadvantages of Hot Sites:

- Very expensive

- Limited on hardware and software choices Advantages of Warm and Cold Sites:

- Less expensive

- Availability for longer timeframes because of the reduced costs

- Practical for proprietary hardware or software use Disadvantages of Warm and Cold Sites:

- Not immediately available

- Operational testing not usually available

- Resources for operations not immediately available Backup tapes or media should be tested periodically on the equipment it will be eventually used on during a recovery.

Rolling hot sites or mobile hot sites are another option; the back of a large truck or trailer is turned into a data processing or working area.

Important data should be backed up to an onsite area and an offsite area.

Onsite backup information should be stored in a fire-resistant safe. The procedures for backing up and restoring data should be easily accessible and comprehensible.

Master files should be updated an sensible intervals and transaction files should be preserved to coincide with those master files.

A disk-shadowing subsystem uses two physical disks and the data is written to both at the same time for redundancy purposes. This subsystem performs data-mirroring functionality. If one disk fails the other is readily available.

Disk-duplexing involves more than one disk controller. If one disk controller fails, the other is ready and available.

Backup Types:

- Incremental backup – a procedure that backs up only those files that have been modified since the previous backup of any sort. It does remove the archive attribute.

- Differential backup – a procedure that backs up all files that have been modified since the last full backup. It does not remove the archive attribute.

- Full backup – a procedure that backs up all files, modified or not, and removes the archive attribute.

Incremental backups finish more quickly than differential backups, but they take longer to restore because each incremental backup has to be restored since the last full backup.

Transaction Redundancy:

- Electronic Vaulting

- Copy of modified file is sent to a remote location where an original backup of the file is stored

- Transfers bulk backup information

- Batch process of moving data

- Remote Journaling

- Moves the journal or transaction logs to a remote location, not the actual files

- Parallel processing of transactions to an alternate site

- Communication line used to transmit data as it is generated Hierarchical Storage Management (HSM) provides continuous online backup functionality. It combines hard disk technology with the cheaper and slower optical or tape jukeboxes. The HSM system dynamically manages the storage and recovery of files, which are copied to storage media devices that vary in speed and cost.

Storage Area Network (SAN) is made up of several storage systems that are connected together to form a single backup network. A SAN is a networked infrastructure that allows several systems to be connected to any storage device. Switches provide a switching fabric that allows for several devices to be able to communicate with back-end storage devices and provides redundancy and fault tolerance by not depending upon one specific line or connection. Private channels or storage controllers are implemented so that hosts can access storage devices transparently.

Tape vaulting – data is sent over a serial line to a backup tape system at the offsite facility.The company that is maintaining the offsite facility will maintain the systems and change out tapes when necessary.

Documentation is an essential piece in disaster recovery and business continuity.

Types of Business Continuity tests/drills:

- Checklist test – review business plan for any omissions.

- Structured walk-through test – go over objectives of the plan, discuss scope and assumptions of the plan, review the organization and reporting structure and evaluate the testing, maintenance and training requirements described. Walk through different scenarios of the plan from beginning to end to ensure nothing was left out.

- Simulation test – practice executing the disaster recovery plan based on a specific scenario. The drill will have only those materials that will be available in an actual disaster to portray a more realistic environment.

- Parallel test – done to ensure that the specific systems can perform adequately at the alternate offsite facility. The systems are moved to the alternate site and processing takes place. Results are compared with the regular processing that is done at the original site.

- Full-Interruption test – full-blown drill that takes a lot of planning and coordination.
